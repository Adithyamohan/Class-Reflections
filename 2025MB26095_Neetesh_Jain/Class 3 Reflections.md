# Session 3: Somebody finally explained the sprint to me

18 August 2026 | Neetesh Jain | 2025MB26095

---

Ten years of sprints. Hundreds of them. Nobody has ever told me what the sprint was for, and I never asked, because it arrived as a process rather than as a reason.

The framing here is that Scrum is a labour negotiation. The product manager has to change direction constantly because the market changes constantly, and from the team's side that is being pulled around with no visibility of what is coming. So there is a deal. The team gets one window of guaranteed no change, and at the end of that window everything is open.

That single sentence explains almost every argument I have had at work.

**Why it explains so much.** I have always experienced mid sprint scope changes as disrespect rather than inefficiency, and I could never say why the reaction was so out of proportion to the actual disruption, which is often small. Now I can. It was not a nuisance. It was the other side breaking the only term of an agreement I was never told existed. I was enforcing a contract I could not name against people who did not know they had signed it.

The two rules sharpen it. You may cancel a sprint entirely with no liability to the team. You may not change scope inside it. Every PM I have worked with has done the reverse without exception. Not one has ever cancelled a sprint. All of them have changed scope inside one.

I now think cancelling would have been better for us than most of those changes were. A cancelled sprint is honest. It says the world moved and this work no longer matters, so let us plan again. A scope change says the world moved and you should absorb it inside a commitment you already made.

**Where the framing needs work.** If this is a negotiation, then like any negotiation it depends on both sides being able to enforce their half. The team's enforcement mechanism is nothing. There is no penalty for a broken sprint. The PM's side is enforced by delivery metrics and the team's side is enforced by goodwill.

So Scrum describes a deal while providing no means of holding one party to it, and the side without enforcement loses in every organisation I have worked in. That is not a criticism of the framing, which I found genuinely useful. It is a note that this framing makes the imbalance visible, which the usual process explanation does not.

A participant added the other limit, which is that Scrum does not fit exploratory work where the standards do not yet exist, because the commitment assumes a predictable output. I agree, and would go further. For our AI work the honest unit is not a two week commitment to deliver, it is a two week commitment to learn something specific. Those look identical on a board and they are not the same promise.

**What my own teams do.** Tell me how you measure me and I will tell you how I behave. We optimise velocity, which means we inflate points, which means velocity measures nothing. Everyone knows this and we still report it every fortnight.

**What I am changing.** I will state a range with the reason for its width rather than a single protective number. Three to six weeks because we do not know how messy the historical data is gives the decider something to act on. Six gives them nothing and quietly removes their choices. I expect this to go badly at first, because organisations prefer numbers they can hold you to.
