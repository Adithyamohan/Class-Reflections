# Session 1: Notes from the other side of the table

25 July 2026 | Neetesh Jain | 2025MB26095

---

I have spent over ten years as the person the product manager comes to. So this session landed differently for me than it seemed to land for the room. The class was being told how to be the PM. I was sitting there recognising PMs I have worked with, most of whom I did not understand until now.

**The postman line.** Without technical depth you are reduced to a postman, carrying messages in both directions without adding anything. I have worked for three postmen and you identify them quickly. They cannot tell you why a request matters, so they cannot help you decide what to sacrifice when the estimate comes back too long. The worst effect is not the delay. It is that you stop telling them the truth, because the truth is complicated and they cannot carry complicated. You give them the version they can deliver.

What the session did not address is the opposite failure. I have also worked for one deeply technical PM and that was worse in a different way, because she used the depth to argue about implementation rather than to make trade offs. Technical fluency is necessary and also dangerous.

**Why management gets paid more.** There was a question about this and I have carried the resentment behind it for a decade. The answer was that management is paid for making more good decisions than bad under uncertainty, and that the value of a decision is not the value of the transaction.

I do not fully accept it. It explains why the pay differs in kind, not the size of the gap, and I notice the explanation comes from people on the higher side of it. But I concede the part that applies to me. In ten years almost every technical decision I have made was reversible within a sprint, and I have never made one where I did not know most of the relevant facts. That is a different job, not the same one paid unfairly.

**The thing I got wrong for ten years.** Someone asked whether this is a thankless job, and the answer was essentially yes, because success gets attributed widely and failure specifically.

My reaction was that engineers say exactly this about ourselves, constantly. Nobody notices the system that does not go down. I have used that sentence in appraisals. If both sides believe they are the unrewarded one, neither of us understands what the other absorbs. I did not know that the PM has no functional escape route. I assumed they had one and were choosing not to use it.

**What this means for my part of the project.** I am carrying the PRD, the prioritisation and the economics, all of which I would previously have treated as inputs handed to me.

The habit I want to break is my estimation reflex. Asked how long something takes, I give a number that protects me, and I have always called that realism. From this side, a padded estimate is not safe, it is a decision I am taking on someone else's behalf without telling them. If the real answer is three weeks and I say five, I have quietly killed every option that only works at three. Ten years of being held to estimates has made that padding close to automatic, and I do not yet know if I can stop.
