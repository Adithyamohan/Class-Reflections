# Reflection: Session 3

18 August 2026 | Gangrade Pranjal Jitendra | 2025MB26023

---

The faculty banned the unqualified word "user". Name the stakeholder first, then build the persona.

I went back through my current engagement's requirement documents that evening. "User" appears constantly and means at least four different people depending on the paragraph: the consultant writing a status report, the manager reading it, the practice director reading the roll up, and sometimes the client. Nobody has ever queried this, because everybody reads it as whichever person they had in mind. We have all been agreeing with each other about different things.

The part I had not seen is that naming does more than remove ambiguity. It surfaces conflicts of interest. Once you write "the Engagement Manager wants this", it becomes obvious that the Engagement Manager is the person being assessed by what we are building, and may not want it at all.

**The argument I would have lost.** A participant argued that a PM answers to management for revenue and vision, and that investing in people pushes you into program manager territory. I would have argued that side. It is close to how I justify my own focus: the outcome is mine, the people belong to resourcing. The faculty's answer was that a project team disperses while a product team continues, and a vision without a team that shares it leaves you alone in a corner.

What settled it was the question he closed with. Would your former people work for you again at half the salary? I have worked with well over a hundred people. I think three would. That is not a good number and it follows directly from treating the team as someone else's concern.

**Where I think the framing is too strong.** The caterpillar and butterfly test for transformation is vivid, but the faculty half conceded it when challenged with the athlete analogy, agreeing that 94 to 96 percent is harder than 40 to 60. Once you accept that, the test has dissolved into where you are standing.

I would keep the sandbagging observation instead. If giving management twenty percent this year guarantees a demand for twenty more next year, rational people release five and bank the rest. I have watched this for eight years without naming it. It is the best explanation I have for why our improvement numbers are so suspiciously consistent.

**A number I intend to use.** The product developer historically captures about twenty percent of the value created and the customer keeps eighty, so start at twenty as a ceiling and work down. In services we price on effort, day rate times days plus margin. Nobody has ever asked me what the client's value capture looks like, which means we have no idea whether we are undercharging or overcharging relative to value delivered.

**Revised from session 1.** I resisted the claim that the team belongs to the product owner. I still think the control is not mine. I no longer think that excuses me from the consequence.
