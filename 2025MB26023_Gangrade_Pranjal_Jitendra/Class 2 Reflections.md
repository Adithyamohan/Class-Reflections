# Reflection: Session 2

"A product serves N unknown customers. A project serves one."

I stopped taking notes after that. Everything I have built in eight years has been a project. A client asks, we scope, we build for that client, we hand over, we move on. I have been calling this delivery experience and quietly assuming it was adjacent to product experience. It is not adjacent, it is a different discipline using the same tools. I could feel myself heading for exactly the trap the faculty described, which is proposing my own firm's specific problem as a product. The test is easy to apply now. Could a second firm, whose processes I know nothing about, use this? If it needs a conversation first, it is a project.

**The layering model is the way out.** Generic core, industry variant, customer specific layer on top. What was new was treating it as a discipline rather than a diagram. Protect the core, and if a customer pulls you somewhere only they want, either keep it separate, or make them pay the full unshared cost, or walk away. I have never seen anyone walk away, and I will not be the first. But honest pricing for unshared work is available to me, and I have never framed a custom request that way to a client.

**Where I push back.** Defer decisions as late as possible, and do not pretend certainty. Both are right and they collide in my world. Clients buy fixed price statements of work, which are documents whose purpose is to pretend to a certainty nobody has. When I defer, the client does not read it as judgement, they read it as the vendor not knowing what they are doing. The workable version is deferring internally while being explicit about which things are locked and which are open. I can tell a client the room layout is fixed and the paint colour is not. I cannot say everything is still being considered.

**The one that rearranged something.** "Beta is only halfway." The faculty's example was a card product planned for June that shipped the following February because they scoped authorisation and forgot settlement. The lesson he drew was about energy, not scoping. Do not sprint to beta and leave the team with nothing for the half still coming.

I have burned teams this way more than once. Go live is treated as the finish line, everyone pushes through a brutal four weeks, and then hypercare arrives with nobody left. The client's lasting impression of the engagement forms during exactly the period when my team is most depleted. I have blamed the hypercare staffing model for this. It is a pacing decision I made twelve weeks earlier.

**To test.** Trace one scope change from last quarter across every artifact. I expect it to be visible in the change order and the build, and absent from the test scripts and handover.
