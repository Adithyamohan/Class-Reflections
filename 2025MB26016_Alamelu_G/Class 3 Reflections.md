# Reflection: Session 3

18 August 2026 | Alamelu G | 2025MB26016

One thing I am starting to notice from these classes is that product management is much more about judgement than I initially thought. This session especially made me think about how easy it is to focus on features, frameworks and execution, while missing the actual progress the product is supposed to create.

**The point that stayed with me.** "Progress is more important than features." I liked this because it sounds obvious, but it is actually easy to get wrong. We can keep adding features and still not improve the product in a meaningful way. The progress has to be something agreed with the stakeholders and something that actually matters to the people using the product. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

The discussion around personas also made sense to me. We casually use words like "user" or "customer", but those words can hide a lot of differences. A student, parent, faculty member or employee can all be users of the same product but have completely different needs and situations. So qualifying the user properly is probably important before deciding what the product should actually do. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

**Where I push back.** I agree with the idea of transformation being a big jump rather than just incremental improvement, but I am not sure the distinction is always so clear in companies. A lot of initiatives are called transformation even when they are really just making an existing process faster or cheaper. The caterpillar and butterfly example made the difference easy to understand, but in practice there may be many steps between the two. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

What I did find useful was the warning to be honest with ourselves about the size of the change. A 5% improvement can still be valuable, but calling it transformation does not automatically make it one.

**The product vs project point came back again.** The class reinforced that a product is meant for multiple unknown customers, while a solution designed only for one organisation is really a project. I found the idea of layering useful here. Start with a generic core, then add an industry or domain layer, and only then add customer-specific customisation where required. That seems like a practical way to avoid allowing one customer's requirements to completely change the core product. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

I also liked the point that sometimes the right decision is to say no to a customer. That is probably one of the harder parts of product management because there is always pressure to keep the customer happy. But if every customer gets a different version, the product slowly turns into a collection of projects.

**The thing I found most relevant to AI.** AI is reducing the cost and speed of development, which also means customers can increasingly build things themselves. Earlier, having domain knowledge and an experienced team could be a strong competitive advantage. Now AI is making some of that knowledge much more accessible. So simply knowing how to build something may not be enough of an advantage anymore. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

This made me think that for AI products, the product manager has to keep asking what the real differentiator is. If the customer can reproduce the same solution with an LLM and some API calls, then why would they buy our product instead?

**One thing I want to remember.** Frameworks are only decision aids. They can help structure thinking, but they should not become a substitute for thinking. I liked the "steering wheel" analogy because it is easy to fall into the trap of using more and more frameworks and feeling that the decision is therefore more rigorous. Sometimes the simpler framework is probably enough. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

I also want to explore Jobs to Be Done more. Looking at what progress the customer is actually trying to make, instead of starting with personas and features, feels more useful than immediately jumping into a feature list. The customer journey discussion also reminded me that the product experience should be looked at from acquisition all the way to exit, not just the point where the customer is actively using the product. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

**Open question.** With AI making development much cheaper and easier, I am wondering whether product managers will need to focus less on building capabilities and more on finding defensible sources of value. If almost anyone can build the technology, the harder question may become why customers should choose your product specifically.
