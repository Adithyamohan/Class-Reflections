# Reflection: Session 7

12 September 2026 | Alamelu G | 2025MB26016

This class made me realise that product management is not really split into neat chapters. Pricing, customer support, product design, data, AI, and strategy all influence each other. The faculty even mentioned that it is difficult to say something belongs only to Session 5 or Session 6 because the subject is integrated. 

**The point that stayed with me.** We started with the electricity distribution example, and at first I was thinking about it mainly as a customer support problem. But the discussion slowly changed the way I looked at it. Instead of waiting for a customer to complain about a power outage, the product could detect the problem itself, identify the affected area and inform the customer before they even call. This felt like a much bigger change than simply putting an AI chatbot on top of an existing call centre. 

The same example also showed why knowing the customer's business matters. A power distribution company may already have purchased electricity through forward contracts. If the power cannot be distributed because of an outage, that can become an economic loss for the company. So detecting and fixing the problem early could create value beyond just improving customer satisfaction. 

**The reimagining part was probably my favourite.** The idea that the customer should not have to call at all if the product can already know what is happening made me think about how much unnecessary work exists in many services. GPS could identify the customer's location, operational data could identify the outage, and the system could proactively communicate the expected restoration time. The product is then solving the actual problem instead of just making the complaint process faster. 

**Where I slightly disagree.** Proactive systems sound great, but I think they can also create another problem if the information is wrong. In the discussion, there was an example of the system being unsure whether the outage was actually at the customer's location or another nearby area. That means the product has to deal with uncertainty instead of simply giving a confident answer. I think this is especially important for AI products where the customer may assume that the system knows more than it actually does. 

I liked the idea of showing different levels of confidence in the interface. If something is confirmed, show it clearly. If it is uncertain, communicate that uncertainty. And if the system is completely unsure, maybe it should not show the information at all. This feels like a much more practical approach than just adding a generic "AI may make mistakes" disclaimer. 

**The pricing discussion also connected a lot of things for me.** Earlier I would probably have started with cost-plus pricing, but the class made the bigger point that the customer only has a certain amount of value available to share. The product manager has to understand where that value comes from and how much of it can realistically be captured. The faculty suggested using roughly 15–25% of the value created as a starting mental model, with 20% as a reasonable assumption when there is no better evidence. 

What I found interesting was that willingness to pay is not just about the organisation itself. We also need to understand the people making the decision, what motivates them and what outcomes they care about. So even B2B pricing has a human side to it. 

**Another important learning was about being a real product manager.** The faculty repeatedly pushed us to think from the position of the product company, not from the customer's position. If I am selling a solution to an electricity distribution company, I cannot write an answer saying "the electricity company can do this." I am the one creating the product and the opportunity. That responsibility changes the way I should think about the problem. 

**The AI product lifecycle also stood out.** I had been thinking of a product lifecycle mainly as build, launch and maintenance. But AI adds more uncertainty because the underlying technology and external providers can change after launch. A model or API that worked well during testing may change later, and suddenly the product can behave differently. That means the product manager's job does not become easier after launch. In some ways, the monitoring and risk continue for the full life of the product. 

**What I will take forward.** I want to start looking at products more holistically. Instead of asking only whether a feature works, I should ask what happens before and after it, who is paying, what motivates the buyer, what happens if the AI is wrong, and whether the product can create value somewhere else too. The idea of transferability also stayed with me. A solution built for electricity could potentially be adapted for broadband, water or other geographically distributed utilities. 

**Open question.** I am still thinking about how much responsibility should sit with the product manager when the product depends heavily on external AI providers. Even if the model itself is built by someone else, the customer still experiences my product. So if the provider changes the model, pricing or availability, the problem eventually comes back to me. That makes vendor dependency feel like a product-management problem and not just a technical procurement problem.
