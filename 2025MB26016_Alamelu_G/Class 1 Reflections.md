# Reflection: Session 1

**25 July 2026 | Alamelu G | 2025MB26016**

The first thing that stayed with me from the class was that a product manager is not just the person coordinating between business and technology. The discussion started with the usual answers — managing the lifecycle, understanding customer requirements, bridging teams, deciding what to build — but then the bigger point came through: **the product manager owns the problem and the outcome**.

I think I have usually looked at product management more as a coordination role. In projects I have worked on, it is easy to think that once requirements are clear and development is moving, the main job is being done. The class made me think that this is probably too narrow. The PM still owns the outcome even when the actual work is being done by different teams. Also, I liked the point that there is no single perfect answer in management. A lot depends on assumptions, available resources and what we know at that point in time.

**Where I slightly disagree.** The idea of ownership sounds straightforward in theory, but in a real organisation a PM does not always have control over the people, budget or technical resources. So saying “you own the outcome” can sometimes feel a little disconnected from reality. But I think the useful part is that ownership does not necessarily mean controlling everything. It means taking responsibility for making the best decision with whatever is available and influencing people even when you don't have direct authority.

**The story that landed.** The example about an architect proposing a technically better database was interesting. The decision was not made only on the technical merits. The available talent and the ability to maintain the solution later also mattered. That made me realise that a “better technology” is not automatically a better product decision. There are other columns in the decision which may not appear in a technical comparison. The same thinking applies to AI products also, where choosing the most advanced model may not always be the right business decision.

Another thing I noted was the idea of trade-offs. A PM is constantly choosing between features, time, cost, people and talent. So product management seems less about finding the perfect option and more about making a reasonable choice between imperfect options.

**What I will take forward.** When I look at a product or project now, I want to ask myself not only “Can we build this?” but also “Should we build this?”, “What happens after it is built?” and “Who will benefit from it?” I also want to pay more attention to the business model, users, competition and the practical side of maintaining a product, instead of looking at the feature or technology in isolation.

The three ways AI comes into the product world was also something I found useful — using AI to build products, using AI for product-management work, and embedding AI inside the product. I had mostly thought about AI in terms of building features, so the second point especially made me think about how AI could change the actual product management work.

**Open question.** I am still wondering how much ownership a product manager can realistically have in a large organisation where important decisions such as staffing, budgets and technology choices are controlled elsewhere. The class made me think that maybe the real skill is not having complete control, but being able to make good decisions and influence the organisation despite not having all the authority.
