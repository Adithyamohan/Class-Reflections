# Reflection: Session 4

22 August 2026 | Alamelu G | 2025MB26016


The question I kept coming back to in this class was not "Can AI do this?" but "Should AI do this?" We looked at several examples where AI could clearly be added, but that alone does not mean the product becomes better. The starting point should be a hypothesis about the value being created, and then checking the downside if the AI gives the wrong answer. That shift from being excited about the technology to questioning the actual value felt important to me. 

**The point that stayed with me.** AI should earn its place in a product. I think there is a tendency now to add AI because it sounds innovative or because everyone else is doing it. The class made me think that the better question is whether the AI creates incremental value that could not be created as simply by using rules, search, analytics or some other approach. The suggestion to start with the least complex solution and only move towards ML, generative AI or agents when needed made a lot of sense. 

**What I found slightly uncomfortable.** The discussion around AI making decisions was probably the most serious part for me. There is a difference between AI giving a recommendation and AI taking the actual decision. In areas like lending, medical decisions or agriculture, an incorrect recommendation can have a real consequence. So even if the technology is capable, the product manager still has to think about who has the final judgement and who carries the accountability. I liked the idea of keeping a human involved where the consequences are significant rather than assuming automation is always the end goal. 

**The actionable window example landed.** The rain example was simple but effective. Telling a farmer that heavy rain is coming after there is no time left to harvest does not really create much value. The information may be accurate, but it is not useful anymore. The same applies to autonomous driving, where the system has to detect, alert and allow enough time for the human or machine to react. This made me realise that accuracy alone is not enough. Timing, latency and the ability to act on the information are also part of product value. 

**Another thing I noted was tail risk.** Looking only at average performance can hide the cases that matter most. If an AI system gives the wrong recommendation once in a very large number of cases but that one case causes serious harm, the average accuracy does not tell the full story. For AI products, I think this means guardrails and escalation paths are as important as the model performance itself.

**Adoption is a separate problem from value.** I liked the discussion on early adopters because I often think that if a product is clearly better, people will automatically use it. That is not true. Some users are willing to experiment, while others care much more about reliability, support, switching cost and what happens if things go wrong. The car example made this obvious. A very attractive feature may still not be enough if the infrastructure and trust around the product are missing. 

This also connects to AI products inside organisations. Even when employees like the output of an AI tool, adoption can still be low because the tool changes their workflow. Training, integration and the effort of switching from the existing process can become part of the product problem. I think I had been looking at adoption mostly as a communication or change-management issue, but it is actually part of product design.

**The framework discussion also stood out.** The faculty again stressed that frameworks are decision aids, not the decision itself. I think this is becoming clearer across the classes. There is a temptation to keep adding frameworks because it makes the analysis look more structured, but if two frameworks are asking the same questions and not changing the decision, they are probably just adding ceremony. 

**What I will take forward.** For our own product work, I want to start by writing down the hypothesis of where AI is supposed to create value and then actively try to break that hypothesis. I should also ask what happens when the system is wrong, who is affected, whether the output is actionable in time, and whether the user actually has a reason to adopt it. The idea of testing small before scaling also feels especially important for AI because the real-world conditions may be very different from the conditions under which the product was designed.

**Open question.** I am still thinking about where the right boundary is between automation, AI assistance and human judgement. It seems easy to say "keep a human in the loop", but that does not solve everything. The human may start trusting the AI too much and simply approve whatever it suggests. So the harder product-management question may be how to design the workflow so that the human is actually exercising judgement rather than just becoming a rubber stamp for the model.
