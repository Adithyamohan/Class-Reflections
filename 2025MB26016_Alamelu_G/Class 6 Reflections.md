# Reflection: Session 6

5 September 2026 | Alamelu G | 2025MB26016

This class made me think more about the difference between having an idea and actually being able to build a product around that idea. We spent quite a bit of time discussing our assignment, but I found the discussion useful because it connected directly to how we should think as product managers.

**The point that stayed with me.** "Don't start with the technology, start with the customer's problem." This has come up in different forms in the earlier classes too, but I think I understood it better this time. We should first identify who the customer is, what problem they have, whether the problem happens repeatedly, and what measurable value we can create. Only then should we think about whether AI is the right solution. 

The idea of the **art of possibility** was also interesting. Sometimes the biggest limitation is that we don't know what technology can actually do today. The example of converting textbooks into audio made this clear. Something that may not have been economical or practical earlier can become possible as technology changes. So as a product manager, I think we need to keep updating our understanding of what is possible instead of assuming that today's constraints will always exist. 

**The part I found most useful.** The faculty asked us to reimagine existing workflows instead of simply adding AI to the current process. Looking at the problem as a black box was a good way to think about it. We can keep the same output but question whether the inputs and internal process need to remain the same. Maybe some inputs can be removed, combined or replaced, or maybe new inputs can improve the quality of the output. 

This made me think that re-engineering a workflow may actually be more important than adding an AI feature to it. If we simply take an inefficient process and put AI in the middle, we may just be making the same inefficient process faster.

**Where I slightly disagree.** The idea of building something better than an existing product is definitely useful, but I think "better" depends heavily on the customer segment. A product does not necessarily need to beat every existing competitor on everything. It may be better for a specific use case or a specific group of customers. The discussion around the Uber example was still useful because it reminded us not to build a simple clone just because it is easy to explain. 

**The documentation discussion was also important.** I usually think of documents like PRDs as something created mainly for communication with the team. But the class made me see them more as a way to make our thinking explicit. Requirements should be written from the stakeholder's perspective and different stakeholders should be clearly identified. Technical details such as system capacity belong in the product specification rather than being mixed into the stakeholder requirement itself. 

I also liked the instruction not to simply remove sections from a standard template because they don't apply. Instead, we should mention that a section is not applicable and explain why. That forces us to think about whether we actually considered the issue rather than just forgetting it. 

**Another thing I noted.** The class connected data choices with product investment decisions. The way we collect and use data affects the quality of the product, not just the technical side of the model. Poor or biased data can lead to a model that looks good but is not useful in the real world. So product managers need to care about data even when they are not personally building the data pipelines. 

The discussion on hidden human emotions also stood out. A product can have all the right features and still fail because we don't understand what people actually feel about using it. Sometimes people give indirect signals that are not visible in a feature list. That seems especially relevant for AI because trust and comfort can matter as much as functionality. 

**What I will take forward.** For our own project, I want to spend more time challenging the problem statement before jumping into the solution. I want to look at the existing workflow, question its assumptions, identify what is actually painful or repetitive, and then decide where AI can genuinely improve it. I also need to be more disciplined about defining stakeholders and keeping the terminology consistent across the team.

**Open question.** I am still thinking about how much reimagining is too much. At some point, changing the workflow so much can make the solution difficult for people to adopt. So there seems to be a balance between creating something genuinely new and keeping enough familiarity that customers are willing to use it.
