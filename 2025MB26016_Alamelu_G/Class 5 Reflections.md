# Reflection: Session 5

29 August 2026 | Alamelu G | 2025MB26016


This class made me think more carefully about how we actually find opportunities for AI. The first reaction is usually to see a process and ask where AI can be added. The faculty's approach was almost the opposite first understand where there is real value, and only then decide whether AI is the right way to create it. Just using AI does not automatically create additional value, and customers will not pay more simply because something is called AI.

**The point that stayed with me.** The idea of looking at the workflow from the customer's point of view was useful. What is the customer actually trying to get done? Can we make it easier, faster or better? The example of Google not putting ads on the search home page also made me think about how sometimes the product has to focus on the customer's job rather than the company's opportunity to make money. The customer wants to finish the job, not necessarily spend more time inside the product. 

I also noted the importance of evidence. It is easy to say that a particular AI feature is useful, but as a product manager we should be able to show what changed because of it. Experiments, results and actual evidence should support the decision instead of just saying "I know this will work." 

**Where I push back.** I agree with the idea of not adding AI everywhere, but in organisations there can be pressure to show that a product is using AI because it is seen as strategic. Sometimes a relatively small improvement may still be called an AI initiative because of that pressure. The better approach, I think, is to separate the technology story from the actual business outcome and be honest about whether the improvement is material.

**One example that made sense.** The example of building a user interface for something that changes only once every two or three years was quite practical. It showed that even automation may not be worth doing when the problem is not frequent or painful enough. We sometimes over-engineer because we are focusing on what can be built rather than whether it is worth building. 

**The build vs buy vs partner discussion was also interesting.** With AI, the choice is not simply whether we can build something ourselves. We have to think about whether the capability is important enough to own, whether we should buy it, use an API or partner with someone. There is also a risk of becoming too dependent on a vendor, especially if that vendor controls the model, pricing or future product roadmap. 

This made me think differently about APIs. Using an external model can make development much faster, but it can also create dependency. If the model provider changes its pricing, availability or capabilities, our product can be affected. So the product manager has to think beyond today's technical solution and also consider the longer-term ownership of the capability.

**Another thing I found important was human judgement.** We discussed how AI should not automatically be allowed to make decisions just because it can. Sometimes the right design is to have the AI support the human and escalate cases where there is uncertainty. But even then there is a risk of "human fatigue" where people stop questioning the AI and just accept whatever it says. That means the product design has to make the human actually use their judgement rather than simply approve the AI output. 

**What I will take forward.** For our product idea, I want to start with the problem and workflow before thinking about the AI solution. I will try to ask: Is the problem frequent enough? Is it painful enough? What value will actually change? What evidence would prove that value? And if AI is used, is it the simplest suitable solution or are we using a more complicated technology just because it is available?

**Open question.** The biggest question I have after this class is about competitive advantage. If AI models and capabilities are becoming easier and cheaper for everyone to access, then it may become difficult to differentiate just through technology. The real advantage may have to come from understanding the customer's workflow better, integrating the solution properly and creating enough trust and value that the customer does not feel the need to build it themselves.
