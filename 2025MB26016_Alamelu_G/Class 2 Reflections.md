# Reflection: Session 2

1 August 2026 | Alamelu G | 2025MB26016

"A product is for N number of unknown customers. A project can start with one known customer."

This was probably the point that made me rethink the most. I have worked on many things where we solve a problem for a particular business or stakeholder and it is easy to start thinking of that as a product. But the class made the distinction much clearer. A product may start from one known problem, but the problem has to be generalised for a larger set of customers. Otherwise it is still basically a project.

I also liked the product lifecycle discussion because it was not presented as a straight line. Discover, define, strategize, plan, build, launch, grow and then possibly renew or retire. The part I noted for myself was that a product keeps learning and changing. I usually think of launch as the major finish line, but the class made it seem more like one checkpoint in a much longer learning loop.

**The one that stayed with me.** "Don't pretend certainty." I think this is actually difficult in organisations because people want a roadmap and dates even when there are still many unknowns. The idea of deferring decisions as late as possible, without affecting the goals, made sense to me. Some decisions are expensive to change later and those need to be made early, but smaller decisions can stay open until there is more information. The house construction example made this easy to understand the structure needs to be decided early, but the paint colour does not.

**Where I push back.** I agree with deferring decisions, but I think there is a fine line between strategic deferring and just avoiding a difficult decision. The faculty also pointed out that stakeholder expectations need to be aligned early. That part is important because otherwise a decision which we think is still open may already be assumed to be final by someone else. So I think the practical version is to clearly communicate what is fixed, what is still open and when it will be decided.

**The launch lesson.** The example of the credit card product was useful. The team initially thought beta meant they were almost done, but they had only looked at authorization and later realised settlement was also necessary. The actual launch happened much later than expected. The lesson I took from this was "beta is only halfway." It is easy to use beta as a psychological finish line and push the team very hard to get there. But if there is still a lot of work after beta, the team may already be exhausted. I liked this because it connects product planning with the human side of execution as well.

Another point I noted was that a product does not need to be perfect before launch. It needs to be "good enough" for the particular stage. That sounds simple, but deciding what good enough actually means is probably one of the harder jobs for a product manager. There will always be one more feature or improvement that can be added.

**What I will take forward.** I want to start looking at decisions more as connected decisions rather than isolated ones. The discussion on artifacts and traceability made sense here. If a product decision is made, it should show up wherever that decision has an impact architecture, design, engineering, testing, documentation, etc. A decision that exists only in a meeting and disappears afterwards is not really useful.

I also found the distinction between feedback and learning interesting. Feedback is something someone gives you, while learning can come from what you observe or understand yourself. I think both are needed during product development because especially with new or AI-based products, the first version will rarely be the final version.

**Open question.** I am still thinking about how product managers decide when they have enough evidence to move forward. We can keep experimenting and learning, but at some point someone has to say "this is good enough, let's launch." That decision seems to involve judgement more than any framework, and I think understanding how to make that judgement will probably be one of the more useful parts of this course.
